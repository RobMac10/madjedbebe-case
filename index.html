Miss put a jar of sand on the bench and turned the lights off.

She said that is how the scientists treated it. Sand from deep in the ground at Madjedbebe, a rock shelter on Mirarr Country in Arnhem Land, was taken out and kept in the dark, because sunlight resets the clock.

A grain of quartz slowly stores up energy while it is buried. In a laboratory it can be made to give that energy back as a tiny flash of light. More light, longer underground. So the date is not written on anything. It is counted, grain by grain.

The team who dug there say people were living at Madjedbebe around 65,000 years ago. Another team says the numbers do not fit, and that termites and rain can move things down through sand. The Mirarr people agreed to the dig, and they had the right to stop it.

I wrote 65,000 in my book. Then I put a question mark next to it.

Madjedbebe: dating the sand

How old is Madjedbebe, and how sure can anyone be?

What is agreed

Madjedbebe is a rock shelter on the western edge of the Arnhem Land plateau in the Northern Territory, on Mirarr Country. It was formerly known as Malakunanja II. It sits on Aboriginal land inside the Jabiluka mining lease, which is surrounded by Kakadu National Park.

The site was excavated in 1973 and in 1989. Sediments from the 1989 dig were dated to about 60,000 to 50,000 years ago, using thermoluminescence and optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) dating. Those ages were argued over, mainly because of doubts about whether the artefacts had stayed in the layers where they were found.

A team led by archaeologist Chris Clarkson of the University of Queensland dug at Madjedbebe again in 2012 and 2015. The dating was led by Zenobia Jacobs of the University of Wollongong. The work was done under an agreement with the Gundjeihmi Aboriginal Corporation, which represents the Mirarr. Under it, the Mirarr had a right to stop the dig at any time, control over the artefacts, and a final say on the findings announced.

The team’s results were published in the journal Nature on 20 July 2017. The paper said that human occupation began around 65,000 years ago. The lowest dense band of artefacts was given a modelled start of about 65,000 years ago and an end of about 52,700 years ago, each with a margin of error of several thousand years.

OSL dating does not date a stone tool. It measures how long ago grains of sand were last in sunlight. A date for a tool depends on the tool staying in the same layer as the sand around it.

In 2018 archaeologists James O’Connell, Jim Allen and colleagues published a paper in PNAS (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences). They called the ages anomalous, and argued that artefacts could have moved down through the sand. The argument has continued in later papers. This file records both sides. It does not settle the question.

Three witnesses

The same three questions for each: What did they say they saw? · Could they really have seen it? · Why might they say it? A period portrait is used where one exists and is free to reuse. Otherwise you will see a plain silhouette, not a likeness. Tap a picture to enlarge it.

Chris Clarkson, Zenobia Jacobs and colleagues

Archaeologists and a dating specialist · Excavated Madjedbebe in 2012 and 2015 · Nature, 20 July 2017

The team who dug the site, dated the sand and published the 65,000-year result.

Source. C. Clarkson, Z. Jacobs, B. Marwick and others, “Human occupation of northern Australia by 65,000 years ago,” Nature 547 (2017), pp. 306–310. Quoted from the published abstract and main text. The image is a document silhouette, not a facsimile.

What they said. “Human occupation began around 65,000 years ago, with a distinctive stone tool assemblage including grinding stones, ground ochres, reflective additives and ground-edge hatchet heads.” They wrote that this “sets a new minimum age for human arrival in Australia,” and that “the total age uncertainties are only 3–4 kyr” (thousand years) for the sand samples linked to the lowest dense band of artefacts.

Could they really have seen it? They were there, layer by layer, and they measured the sand themselves. They are the best-placed witnesses to what was in the ground. But a luminescence date is a date for when sand was last in sunlight. Linking that date to a stone tool depends on the tool not having moved. Their paper argues that it had not moved, using pieces of stone that fit back together and other tests.

Why might they say it? To report a major result, carefully, in a journal that checks papers before they are published. Scientists also argue for their own methods, and a very old date is big news. Students can ask both questions: how good is the evidence, and who has a stake in the answer?

James O’Connell, Jim Allen and colleagues

Archaeologists · Did not dig at Madjedbebe in 2012 or 2015 · PNAS, August 2018

Critics who read the published data and argued that the 65,000-year date does not fit the wider evidence.

Source. J. F. O’Connell, J. Allen and others, “When did Homo sapiens first reach Southeast Asia and Sahul?”, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 115, no. 34 (2018), pp. 8482–8490. Quoted from the section on Madjedbebe. The image is a document silhouette, not a facsimile.

What they said. “In the context of Sahul and the wider region, these ages are anomalous.” They wrote that “these mismatches demand critical review,” and that sandy deposits in the tropics are open to many kinds of disturbance, “most notably, bioturbation by termites.” They also pointed to a radiocarbon date of about 13,000 years on charcoal from the same depth in the 1989 dig, which the excavators had called intrusive.

Could they really have seen it? They did not dig in 2012 or 2015. They worked from the published reports and from comparisons with many other sites across the region. That is a real kind of evidence: if one site is far older than all the others, it is fair to ask why. But a comparison with other sites cannot prove what happened inside this one.

Why might they say it? To test a big claim, which is how science is meant to work. They also had their own view that people reached Sahul (Australia and New Guinea joined at low sea levels) closer to 50,000 years ago. Students can ask whether a critic who already holds a different date is more careful, or less open, or both.

Gundjeihmi Aboriginal Corporation

Represents the Mirarr Traditional Owners · Partner in the 2012 and 2015 excavations · Media release, 19 July 2017

The organisation of the people whose Country the site is on, and who controlled the terms of the dig.

Source. Gundjeihmi Aboriginal Corporation, “Kakadu site of Australia’s earliest home,” media release, 19 July 2017, mirarr.net. Quoted from the published release. No photograph of any person is used. The image is a document silhouette, not a facsimile.

What they said. “A team of archaeologists and dating specialists have new proof that Aboriginal people have been in Australia for at least 65,000 years — much longer than the 47,000 years believed by some archaeologists.” The release described the agreement as “giving access to country for the research team and providing the Mirarr with unprecedented control over the operations of the excavation and its findings.” Its chief executive, Justin O’Brien, said: “These findings reinforce the need for the highest level of conservation and protection for this site.”

Could they really have seen it? The Corporation did not do the dating. Its release reports the scientists’ result. It could speak with authority about the agreement, about who controlled the dig, and about the place itself.

Why might they say it? To share important news about Mirarr Country, to show a model of research done in partnership, and to argue for protecting the site, which lies inside a uranium mining lease. Students can ask how a result reads differently when it comes from the Traditional Owners rather than from a journal.

Why it still matters

A number like 65,000 travels fast. It appears in speeches, in classrooms and on signs. The file is open because the number came from sand, not from a label, and because scientists are still testing it. Who controls a dig, who announces the result, and who gets to say how old a place is, are questions about the present as well as the past.

The typed page is a constructed student voice in a science room. The narrator is not a historical person. The opener does not quote any scientist or Traditional Owner. The facts in it (the dark, the grains, 65,000, the right to stop the dig) are from the agreed file.

Question. Which sentences in the notebook can you check against the agreed file, and which are only the narrator’s feelings?

2. What does a luminescence date measure?

Optically stimulated luminescence measures the energy stored in quartz grains since they were last in sunlight. Samples are collected so that light cannot reach them. The date belongs to the sand. It becomes a date for a stone tool only if the tool and the sand have stayed together.

Question. What would have to be true for the date of the sand to also be the date of the tool?

3. The charcoal that did not fit

A piece of charcoal from about the same depth in the 1989 dig gave a radiocarbon date of about 12,800–13,000 years. The excavators said it had probably fallen or blown in during the dig. Their critics said that answer was not explained. Radiocarbon dating cannot reach back much beyond about 50,000 years, so it cannot test a 65,000-year date directly.

Question. If one date disagrees with eighteen others, when is it fair to set it aside, and when should it make you doubt the rest?

4. Deeper still?

Below the dense band, the published data list hundreds of artefacts in sand dated to about 65,000 to 87,000 years. The excavators thought most had settled down from above. Some newspapers in July 2017 reported occupation “up to 80,000 years ago.” The Nature paper itself argued for about 65,000.

Question. Why might a newspaper headline choose a bigger number than the scientists’ own paper?

5. Who controls a dig?

Under the 2012–2015 agreement, the Mirarr could stop the dig, controlled the artefacts, and had a final say on what was announced. Reports at the time said the artefacts were to be returned to the Mirarr. The site is inside the Jabiluka uranium mining lease.

Question. How might an agreement like this change what a team is allowed to find, keep and say? Is that a weakness, a strength, or both?

6. Sure, maybe, or a guess?

Sure: Madjedbebe is on Mirarr Country; it was dug in 1973, 1989, 2012 and 2015; the 2017 Nature paper dated first occupation to around 65,000 years ago using OSL; other archaeologists dispute that age. Maybe: whether the artefacts stayed in place; whether people arrived earlier still. A guess: an exact year, or what anyone at Madjedbebe was thinking. The opener is not a source.

Question. Write a finding of no more than five sentences, and mark each sentence sure, maybe, or a guess.

Source conflicts, flagged

Nature’s online abstract and the published article word the toolkit slightly differently (“grindstones … groundedge axes” and “grinding stones … ground-edge hatchet heads”); this file quotes the published version. The dispute over Madjedbebe is live: O’Connell, Allen and colleagues (2018) and Williams and colleagues (Geoarchaeology, 2021) argue for disturbance; the excavators and others have replied. Students should not be told the matter is settled either way. “47,000 years” in the Corporation’s release refers to the younger estimates favoured by some archaeologists at the time.
